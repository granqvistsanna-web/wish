# Chapter 1 — Arrival

She counted the steps from where the transport left her to where the station began. Four hundred and twelve. The road had ended a kilometer back, dissolved into cracked hardpan and scrub, and she had walked the rest with her bag over her good shoulder and the sun pressing down like a hand on the back of her neck.

Station 7-Syd rose from the plain in stages. First the module arrays, rows of dark panels angled south, their surfaces coated in a fine layer of dust that nobody had cleaned. Then the low buildings: prefab concrete, sand-colored, blending into the terrain as if the desert were swallowing them. A greenhouse, its panels fogged with condensation. A water tank on stilts. Beyond it all, more modules stretching to the horizon, and beyond that, nothing.

Amma stopped at the perimeter gate. Shifted her bag. Her shoulder sent a line of pain from the old injury down through her arm, and she adjusted the strap without thinking about it. She had stopped thinking about the shoulder the way she had stopped thinking about most things that hurt — by noting the sensation and moving past it.

A woman stood inside the gate. Short gray hair, a face cut by sun and years, a cotton shirt buttoned halfway. Arms crossed, a resting position, the way some people stand when they've been standing a long time. No assistant button on her collar. Amma noted it. Everyone in the briefing file wore one.

"Du är teknikern," the woman said. Not a question.

"Amma Wost." She held out her hand.

The woman looked at it. Took her bag instead. Turned and walked into the compound.

"Karin," she said over her shoulder. "Jag sköter stället."

Amma followed. Karin moved through the station the way water moves through a channel — without hesitation, taking the path that already existed. She pointed at buildings as they passed. "Matsal." Dining hall. "Sovbaracker." Sleeping quarters. "Växthus." Greenhouse. The words came in bursts, stripped of everything unnecessary.

They passed a man in the shade of the maintenance shed. White mustache, hands resting on his knees, the weathered look of someone who'd spent decades outdoors. He squinted at Amma as she passed and opened his mouth to speak, but Karin was already turning the corner.

Karin glanced at Amma's hands on the bag strap. Then at her face. Her eyes lingered a beat too long on Amma's skin, her hair.

"Jag skulle inte nämna var du kommer ifrån," Karin said. Quiet. Not looking at her. "Inte för de andra."

I wouldn't mention where you're from. Not to the others.

Amma's throat tightened. She kept walking. "Jag kommer från Capitol."

"Mm," Karin said. The sound held no belief in it.

"Rum fyra," Karin said, stopping at a door. She set Amma's bag inside without entering. "Middagen om en timme."

The room was small. A narrow bed against the wall, a shelf, a window that faced the module arrays. Clean — someone had scrubbed the floor and changed the sheets — but not empty. Not quite. The mattress dipped in the center where another body had worn it down over years. A faded rectangle on the shelf marked where something had stood. And near the headboard, low on the wall where you'd only see them lying down, scratch marks in the concrete.

Amma set her bag on the shelf. Sat on the bed. The mattress gave under her in the wrong places, shaped for someone shorter, someone who slept on her side.

Nobody had said this was Maja's room. Nobody had needed to.

Karin was still in the doorway. "Koppen med den blå kanten," she said. "Den använder vi inte."

Then she was gone.

---

The dining hall smelled of root vegetables and something smoky — wood, maybe, or whatever Viktor was burning in his still behind the greenhouse, if the stories from the briefing file were accurate. A long table filled most of the room. Benches on both sides, a window at the far end letting in the last of the daylight.

Amma paused in the entrance. Read the room.

A man at the head of the table. Viktor, it had to be. White mustache, big hands that trembled around his spoon. He was already talking when she walked in, mid-sentence about something that had happened before she existed. Beside him, two people so alike it took her a moment to sort them: the twins, Elias and Nora. Elias had the narrower face. Nora the darker hair. Both wore small metal pins on their collars — assistant buttons. They ate in silence, side by side, watching Amma with the same measured curiosity.

Across the table: Samuel. She recognized him from the file but the photograph hadn't captured the restlessness, the way he shifted in his seat, the unwashed hair falling across his forehead, the dark circles. The scar was there, a pale line from his neck down over his shoulder and under his shirt collar. He looked up when she entered and gave her a smile that bent to one side and stopped before it reached his eyes.

At the far end of the table, as far from everyone as the room allowed: a woman Amma's briefing file hadn't mentioned. Dark features, black hair pulled back in a tight knot that caught the light. She ate with mechanical precision. Fork to mouth, chew, swallow. Fork to mouth. She did not look up.

There was one empty chair.

Amma sat beside Samuel. It was the open seat.

"Vår nya tekniker," Viktor said, loud enough that it carried past conversation into announcement. He leaned forward, and his mustache moved when he smiled. "Du ser ung ut för din grad." He looked at her hands, her short black hair. "Och du ser inte ut som någon från Capitol."

Amma kept her face still. Took a bowl.

"Vilken grad?"

"Tredje," Amma said. Third grade.

"Tredje." He weighed the word. "Hur många år i fält?"

"Åtta."

"Två?"

"Åtta."

"Sju?"

"Nej. Åtta."

Viktor cupped a hand behind his ear. "Två?"

Samuel coughed into his glass. The twins didn't look up, but Nora's mouth shifted by a fraction.

"Åtta," Amma said, holding up eight fingers.

Viktor nodded, as if she'd finally said something reasonable. "Åtta. Bra. Jag har trettio bara här." He held up his trembling hands and looked at them with something between pride and bewilderment, as if they'd done all the work and were now leaving without permission.

"Han har byggt halva farmen," Samuel said to Amma. His Swedish had a coastal softness to it, the vowels stretched differently than Capitol speech. "Or so he'll tell you. Several times."

"Med dessa händer," Viktor said. "Det var bara sand och vind och getter."

"And goats," Samuel said. "Don't forget the goats." He leaned toward Amma, conspiratorial. "I've been here six years. Six. Cassia says I talk too much. She's right. But what else is there? The sand doesn't talk back. The modules don't talk back. Viktor talks but doesn't listen, which is basically the same thing." He caught himself, grinned. "See? Six years."

Amma took a bowl. The stew was thick, root vegetables and something preserved that might have been meat in a previous life. She ate. It was better than what she'd had on the transport, which wasn't saying much.

Samuel watched her eat. He was too practiced to stare, but she felt the peripheral attention of someone who spent his life monitoring signals.

"Varifrån kommer du?" he asked. Where are you from.

"Capitol. Energidepartementets underhållsenhet." She gave the cover: Energy Department, maintenance division.

"Before that?"

"Before Capitol?"

"Everyone's from somewhere before Capitol."

She gave him the safe truth. "I like the smell of mint. I prefer mornings. I've read every maintenance manual twice and I sleep on my right side."

Samuel's crooked smile returned. "That's not what I asked."

"It's what I answered."

He turned his water glass between his fingers. The scar moved when he swallowed. "Maja wasn't much for conversation either," he said. "Toward the end." He said it lightly. It was not light.

The woman at the end of the table continued eating. Fork to mouth. Chew. Swallow. Amma watched her for three cycles before deciding the regularity was too precise to be unconscious. Whoever she was, she had made her body into a clock.

Then the door opened, and the room changed.

---

Amma didn't understand it at first. Viktor straightened on his bench, his spine remembering a younger posture. Karin, who'd been eating with efficient disinterest at the table's far corner, looked up. Nora set down her spoon. And the woman at the end lifted her eyes from her plate for the first time.

Cassia walked in the way weather arrives. The room had been waiting for her without knowing it.

Long curly hair, graying at the temples, pulled into a knot that was already coming loose. A face that was hard to stop looking at. Not beautiful in any way Amma could have predicted, but entirely present. She looked at the room and the room was seen. Light eyes, gray or green in the dim hall, that moved from face to face without rushing. Taking in everything.

She wore the same work clothes as everyone else. On her they looked chosen.

"Förlåt att jag är sen," she said. She took the empty chair next to Amma and sat down close enough that Amma caught a scent before she processed the words. Not soap. Something cleaner than soap. Something mineral.

"Du måste vara Amma."

Not "the new technician." Her name.

"Cassia," Amma said. She'd practiced the pronunciation. It came out fine.

"How was the journey?"

"Long."

"They always are." Cassia served herself stew with the ease of someone who'd done it a thousand times. "The transport drops you where the road ends and you walk the rest. When I arrived, I thought I'd been sent to the wrong coordinates." She ate a spoonful. "I was wrong. There are no wrong coordinates. Only the ones you end up at."

Viktor, emboldened by Cassia's arrival, launched into the story of building the station. His hands swept the air: the early days, the sand, the goats again. Cassia listened with her body turned toward him, her hand touching his shoulder as she reached for the bread. When he exaggerated the farm's production numbers, "Tretton procent av frontens energi," she didn't correct him.

Amma watched. Cassia asked Nora about a sick friend — Nora nodded, said a few quiet words. She asked Samuel if the comm array was holding in the heat. Samuel said something about signal drift and Cassia nodded as though she understood the specifics, which she might have.

She turned to Amma. "How long will your inspection take?"

"Depends on what I find."

"Practical answer." Cassia's mouth moved into something close to a smile. "I like practical answers."

Something tightened beneath Amma's sternum. She reached for her water glass instead.

Li had returned to her plate. Eyes down. Fork to mouth. Whatever had lifted her gaze when Cassia entered had settled back into stillness.

"Who is that?" Amma asked, low enough that only Samuel and Cassia could hear.

Samuel glanced toward the far end. "Li."

He offered nothing else. Cassia said nothing. The question died where it was.

---

Dinner ended the way institutional meals do. People stood when they'd finished, carried bowls to a basin by the wall, dispersed into the cooling evening. Viktor was last, still talking as he stood, his story now about a sandstorm three years ago that had buried the greenhouse door. No one had asked, but Viktor didn't require prompts.

Amma carried her bowl to the basin. Washed it. The blue-rimmed cup sat on a shelf above the basin, apart from the others. She didn't touch it.

"I'll walk you back," Cassia said from behind her. "So you don't get lost."

The station was twelve buildings. You couldn't get lost if you tried. Amma said nothing. They walked out into the desert evening.

The heat was pulling back. The ground still radiated warmth through the soles of her boots, but the air had shifted, opened. Stars were appearing, more than Amma had seen in years. In Capitol, the sky was a gray-orange haze. Here it was black and deep and full of things she'd stopped believing existed.

Their feet crunched on packed sand. The modules hummed, a low constant sound, like blood moving through a body.

"The work here is straightforward," Cassia said. "Twelve modules, standard maintenance rotation. Samuel handles communications. Karin runs the daily operations. Viktor insists on doing more than he should." She paused, chose her next words. "He's been here the longest. This place is his."

They walked. The silence fit too well. Amma lengthened her stride.

"The last technician was good," Cassia said. Her voice dropped, slowed. "Maja. She checked everything twice. Three times." A breath. "She was working on module 7-12 when she died."

Amma's agent-brain registered the opening. The thing she was here for, offered freely. She said the right thing: "What happened?"

"Officially, an electrical fault in the control panel. An accident." Cassia's pace didn't change. "She'd been working at that module for months. Long hours. She didn't talk about what she was finding."

"Findings?"

"I don't know. I asked. She said the module was fine." Cassia looked at the sky. "Then she was dead, and the production report showed an anomaly that hadn't been there before. Or hadn't been reported."

They'd reached Amma's door. Cassia stopped. Turned to face her. In the half-light, her features were softer, less precise, and her eyes were definitely gray, not green.

"If you find anything unusual at the modules," Cassia said. "Anything at all. I'd like you to tell me first."

The words settled on her like dust. "Before the official report?"

"Before anyone."

The hum of the modules filled the pause. A breeze came from the south, carrying sand and the faint chemical sweetness of the solar panels.

"Why?" Amma said.

Cassia held her gaze. "Because the last time something was wrong, no one told me until it was too late." She said it simply. Grief or strategy. Both, maybe. "Can you do that?"

Amma heard herself say yes. She didn't know why. Or she did and wasn't ready to look at it.

Cassia nodded. "Get some sleep. The heat starts early." She touched the doorframe — her fingers, briefly, on the wood — and then she was walking away, gravel crunching under her steps, her loose hair catching what was left of the light.

Amma went inside. Closed the door. Leaned against it.

She stood there for sixty breaths before she moved.

---

The mattress remembered Maja.

Amma lay on her back and felt the dip where Maja had slept — on her left side, from the shape of it, curled slightly. The shoulder ached against the thin padding. It always ached when she lay flat, the old Sector 14 wound pulling at tissue that had healed wrong and would never heal right.

She stared at the ceiling. Prefab panels, seams visible, a water stain in one corner that looked like a coastline. Her mind ran through the day in sequence, the way she'd been trained: every face logged, every word filed, every inconsistency flagged.

Karin: knew where Amma was from. Had looked at her skin, her hands, and known. Had warned her to hide it. That was either kindness or currency.

Viktor: loud, proud, declining. His hands trembled and he didn't try to hide it. The farm was his identity. When the body went, what would be left?

Samuel: dangerous. Not like a weapon. Like a mirror. He saw things and said them. His question about before-Capitol was precision-guided. The remark about Maja was a test: would Amma flinch?

The twins: watching. Unclear what they wanted. Unclear what they knew.

Li: nothing. An absence shaped like a person. Amma had counted her fork-to-mouth cycles during dinner. Every eight seconds. Perfectly regular. Who eats like that?

Cassia.

Amma closed her eyes. Opened them again.

Cassia's voice asking her to report first. Cassia's eyes in the half-light. The scent — mineral, clean, not soap. The way the room had shifted when she entered, every body recalibrating to her presence. The touch on the doorframe, brief and particular, as if the wood were a person's shoulder.

Amma's assignment was to investigate module 7-12's production anomaly. To determine if the previous technician's death was connected. To report to her handler in Capitol via secure channel, using Samuel's communications system without Samuel knowing.

Cassia had asked her to report to her first. Amma had said yes.

She turned on her side — the good side — and her hand found the wall. The scratch marks were near the headboard, low, where you'd reach in the dark without sitting up. She ran her fingers along them. Grooves in the concrete, made with something sharp. Deliberate.

She counted them.

Twelve marks, evenly spaced. Then a gap — wider than the spaces between the others. Then one more.

Thirteen.

She didn't know what it meant. The number pressed against her thoughts, specific and immovable.

Amma pulled her hand back. Placed it on her sternum. Counted her breaths. In for four, hold for four, out for four. The rhythm she'd used since Sector 14, since the explosion that took the full range of motion in her left shoulder and most of her faith in official explanations.

The modules hummed through the wall. The desert cooled. Somewhere outside, Viktor was probably still talking.

She counted the scratch marks again in the dark. Twelve. A gap. Thirteen.

Whatever Maja had been counting, she'd finished. Or been stopped.

Amma closed her eyes and waited for sleep the way she waited for everything — with control, and without hope.
