# Chapter 2 — The Modules

She woke in someone else's shape.

The mattress held Maja's impression and Amma had slept in it, or near it, her body fitting into grooves worn by another woman's hips and shoulders. The shoulder — her own, the bad one — had stiffened overnight. She rolled it against the mattress and the joint ground like something mechanical that needed oil.

Four thirty-seven. She knew without checking. Her body kept time the way other people breathed.

The farm was already moving. Pipes groaned in the walls. Somewhere outside, the greenhouse vents cycled open with a sound like a long exhalation. Footsteps on gravel. A door. She lay still and listened to the station wake up around her.

She didn't dream. She never dreamed. That was a thing she told herself, and like most things she told herself, it was functional rather than true.

---

The kitchen smelled of coffee and something baked. Karin was at the counter with her back turned, pouring from a metal pot. She didn't look up when Amma entered.

"Bread's on the table. Modules today. Start with the south bank and work north."

Amma sat. The bread was dense, dark, still warm. The coffee was stronger than yesterday's — a different kind, ground finer. She noticed this and said nothing.

"How far is the south bank?" Amma asked.

"Twenty minutes on foot. Fifteen if you don't stop."

"I'll take a toolkit."

"Already by the door." Karin set a cup in front of her. Not Maja's cup. A different one, chipped in a different place. "Eat," she said. "The heat starts early."

Amma ate. The bread tasted of grain and something faintly sweet, an herb she couldn't name. Outside the window the sky was white and blank, already building toward the day's assault.

"Tal will probably follow you," Karin said.

Amma looked up. Karin's back was still turned.

"He followed Maja too. Cassia talks to him about it. It doesn't help."

"Who is Tal?"

"Arrived three months ago. Sol's son." Karin turned the tap and rinsed her hands. "Sol worked the modules before Viktor. Before all of it. He's dead now."

"And Tal?"

"Follows people." Karin dried her hands on a rag, folded it, placed it beside the sink. "Don't let him get in the way of your work."

She left. The kitchen settled into its own quiet.

---

The walk to the modules was twenty-three minutes. Amma counted every one.

The path cut through scrub and dry grass, the kind that crackled underfoot like paper. The station shrank behind her. Ahead, the modules grew from the flat line of the horizon — thirteen metal towers, evenly spaced, their surfaces catching the early sun and throwing it back as blunt white light. Heat came off the ground in waves she could see. Her short black hair stuck to the back of her neck. She should have found something to tie it with.

Her toolkit hung from her right hand. The weight pulled the shoulder and she switched hands, then switched back. Her left was weaker than it used to be. Another gift from Sektor 14.

She passed module 1 and opened the main access panel. Standard interface, dusty but functional. She ran diagnostics, took readings, closed the panel. Module 2. Module 3. The work was repetitive and precise and she let it fill her, the way she let counting fill her — a structure to pour herself into so the rest could stay quiet.

Modules 1 through 6 were aging but sound. The technology was two generations behind Capitol's latest, patched and repatched by hands that knew the equipment the way a farmer knows soil. Maja's work. The calibrations were tight, the maintenance logs meticulous. Whoever Maja had been, she'd been thorough.

By module 4, the heat was serious. Amma's shirt stuck to her back. Sweat traced lines down her ribs. The air smelled of hot metal and dry earth and something organic underneath it, vegetal, like the ghost of something that had once grown here.

She was alone. The landscape swallowed sound the way the desert swallowed everything — quietly, completely, without return. The station was a cluster of shapes behind her. The modules stood ahead like sentinels. Between them, nothing.

It was at module 6 that she heard the footsteps.

---

He was twenty meters off, walking a parallel line through the scrub. Not approaching. Not hiding. Just there, the way a shadow is there — present, cast by something, slightly wrong.

Amma stopped. "Can I help you?"

The young man changed course. He was angular, lean, moving with the tight compressed energy of someone who hadn't learned to pace himself. His face was sharp and young and held the kind of hostility that came from something older than himself. He wore work clothes that fit badly, as if they'd been issued for a different body.

"Cassia wants the modules checked in pairs," he said. "Safety protocol."

"I wasn't told about a safety protocol."

"You're new."

He stood with his hands at his sides and didn't blink. Up close, she could see the resemblance to no one — or to everyone. He had the coloring of the south, dark from sun rather than birth, and eyes that watched the way cameras watched. Passive until triggered.

"Tal," he said. Not offering. Identifying.

"Amma."

He fell into step beside her. Slightly behind, slightly to the side. Like an escort who didn't want to admit it. He watched everything — which panels she opened, which readings she noted, the angle of her hands on the diagnostic tools. He watched the way she worked and she felt it on her skin, a steady low-grade irritation like sunburn.

She opened module 7's access panel and ran the standard sequence. Tal leaned against the tower's base, arms crossed, watching.

"How long have you been at the station?" Amma asked.

"Long enough."

"That's what Viktor says too."

"Viktor talks too much."

No humor in it. Not a joke, not deflection. Just a flat closed door. Amma noted it: unlike Samuel's deflections, which were layered and social, Tal's had no bottom. He wasn't hiding something beneath the surface. The surface was the point.

She tried once more. "You replaced Maja?"

Something moved in his face. Small, fast, gone. Like a fish turning below the surface of still water. "I came after Maja," he said. "I didn't replace her."

He said nothing else for the next three modules.

---

Module 7-12 was the last in the row.

The tower looked identical to the others. Same dull metal casing, same access panels, same coolant pipes running up the south face. But Amma felt the difference before she read it. Under her boots, the ground was warmer. Not hot — warmer. As if something beneath the surface was running a low and patient fever.

She opened the main access panel. Standard interface. She ran diagnostics.

The numbers came in and she kept her face still.

Energy output was above the module's rated capacity. Not dramatically — the kind of deviation that a careless technician might dismiss as drift. But Amma was not careless and this was not drift. The production curve showed a clean step-change: a flat line for months, then a sharp increase. Like a light switched on.

She scrolled back through the logged data. The step-change had begun in the weeks after Maja's death.

Not before. After.

Tal was watching her face. "What?"

"Calibration drift," Amma said. Her voice came out level and professional, the way it always did when she lied about technical things. "Normal for equipment this age. I'll need to run extended diagnostics."

Her heartbeat was steady. Sixty-two beats per minute. She'd checked. She always checked.

Tal didn't believe her. She could see it in the way his weight shifted forward, the way his jaw tightened, the way his eyes went from her face to the screen and back. But he didn't press. He stood there with his arms crossed and his disbelief and his silence, and Amma thought: he knows something. Or he thinks he does.

She closed the panel. She'd memorized the numbers. She would not record them in her official notes.

The ground beneath her feet was warm. She stepped back from the module and the warmth faded. Two steps. That was the boundary. Inside it, warm. Outside, normal. She noted this too, in the part of her mind that kept the things she couldn't write down.

"We're done for today," she said.

Tal looked at module 7-12 the way you'd look at a door you couldn't open. Then he turned and walked toward the greenhouse without a word.

---

Amma walked back alone. The sun was angled now, late afternoon, and her shadow stretched ahead of her across the dust like someone walking faster. The toolkit was heavy. Her shoulder burned from the weight of it and the repetitive motions of the inspections, a deep ache that radiated from the joint down into her arm.

She was thinking about the numbers. Seventeen percent above rated capacity. And climbing. Equipment didn't do that. Equipment degraded, wore down, lost efficiency. It didn't wake up after its technician died and start producing more.

Cassia appeared on the path ahead. Coming from the direction of the south modules, a tool bag over her shoulder, her hair catching loose in the wind. The timing was precise enough to be deliberate, casual enough to deny.

"Long day?" Cassia asked.

"Standard inspection."

They fell into step together. The light was amber. The heat had softened but still pressed against Amma's skin, a dull insistent warmth.

"How did the south bank look?" Cassia asked.

"Aging but functional. Maja's maintenance was thorough — the calibrations are tight. Standard upkeep should keep them running."

Cassia nodded. Something shifted behind her eyes at Maja's name, but her pace didn't change. "And 7-12?"

"I'll need to run more diagnostics." True. Evasive. Amma heard her own voice giving careful partial answers and she thought: we are both doing this. Both circling. Both choosing what to reveal and what to hold.

Cassia didn't push. They walked in a silence that was — and this was the problem — comfortable. Amma was used to silence as a weapon. She'd been trained in silence as interrogation technique, as pressure, as the empty space that made people fill it with truths they hadn't meant to tell. This silence wasn't that. This was two people walking through a landscape at the end of a working day. It asked nothing.

She didn't know what to do with it.

"The modules sound different at night," Cassia said. "A low hum. You hear it the first week. After that it disappears. Not because it stops — because your body absorbs it."

"That sounds uncomfortable."

Cassia smiled. It changed her face — not softened it, precisely, but opened it. A door left ajar. "It is, at first. Then it's like a heartbeat that isn't yours."

They walked. The dust turned amber and then orange and then a color Amma didn't have a word for. The module towers cast shadows that stretched across the flats like sundial arms.

"Maja used to sit by the old well," Cassia said. "After shifts. She said the ground was cooler there. The well's been dry since before I came, but Maja said the stone still held something — moisture, or memory, or whatever it is that old things hold." A pause. Her voice didn't change but her steps slowed. "I still walk past it sometimes."

Amma said nothing. It was the right thing. Some sentences aren't looking for answers. They're looking for a witness.

They were close to the station now. The greenhouses reflected the last light. Amma could hear voices — Viktor's, carrying — and the clang of something metal.

"Cassia," she said. "Can I ask you something?"

"Yes."

"What was Maja's actual role here? She's listed as a technician, like me. But her maintenance work goes beyond standard upkeep. She was doing research-level diagnostics on some of these modules."

Cassia looked at her. A long look, the kind that measured something. "Maja was curious," she said. "Curious about the modules, about how they worked, about why they worked the way they did. We're a small station. People grow into the gaps."

It was an answer. It wasn't the answer. Amma let it stand.

---

They arrived at the station together. Tal was there, crossing from the greenhouse toward the water system, a wrench in his hand. He saw them and something in his posture tightened.

"Tal," Cassia said. Her voice was still warm but a new weight had entered it, the kind that comes with authority held lightly enough to be mistaken for kindness. "You were assigned to the water system today."

"I was checking the modules."

"With Amma. Who didn't request company."

Tal's jaw tightened. The wrench turned once in his hand. He looked at Amma with an expression she couldn't read — not anger, exactly. Something closer to betrayal, though she hadn't betrayed anything.

"Your assignment is the water system," Cassia said. "Please do it."

He walked away. His footsteps were hard on the packed earth.

Cassia watched him go. When she turned back to Amma, her expression was the warm, steady one she wore for public consumption. "He's still finding his place."

"Karin said his father worked here. Sol?"

A flicker. Brief. "Sol was here before my time. He worked the modules. He understood them." She paused. "He died."

"How?"

"I don't know the details." Cassia picked up her tool bag from where she'd set it down. "I'll see you at dinner."

She walked inside. Amma stood in the last of the light and thought about the things Cassia chose to say and the things she chose not to say, and how the gap between them was exactly the shape of a lie.

---

Dinner was quieter than the night before. Viktor was subdued — a headache, he said, pressing his palm to his temple. Samuel filled the space that Viktor left, talking about a signal degradation in the eastern relay. Karin said: "Eat." The twins ate. Li did not look up.

Tal sat at the far end of the table, as far from Cassia as the room allowed. He ate in silence, his shoulders drawn in, his focus on his plate. Once, Amma caught him looking at her across the table. His eyes were flat and hard and young and she held his gaze for three seconds before he looked away.

Samuel leaned toward Amma. "How was the inspection tour with your shadow?"

"I don't know what you mean."

"Tal," Samuel said. "He followed Maja too. Every day for the first month she was here. Then she told him something — nobody knows what — and he stopped." The crooked smile. "Maybe she threatened him. She was capable of that."

"Capable of what?"

"Making you feel small without raising her voice." Samuel picked at his bread. "She had this look. Like the question you'd just asked was so far beneath her that answering it would be an act of charity."

Amma almost smiled. "You liked her."

"Everybody liked Maja," Samuel said. Then, quieter: "Everybody except Maja."

He didn't explain. He didn't need to.

---

Night. The room. The shoulder, the mattress, the small desk under the window.

Amma spread her notes across the desk's surface. The official readings from modules 1 through 6 — clean, standard, unremarkable. And then the numbers she carried in her head. Module 7-12.

She wrote them down now, on a separate sheet she would not include in any report. The production curve was unmistakable. Flat for months. Then, in the week after Maja died, a step-change. Output climbing past rated capacity. Seventeen percent above baseline. And the most recent readings — today's — showed the climb continuing. Eighteen point four percent.

Equipment didn't do this. Equipment was metal and wire and circuitry and it obeyed the laws of thermodynamics and entropy. It wore down. It degraded. It did not wake up.

She thought about the ground beneath module 7-12. The warmth that stopped two steps from the tower's base. She thought about the organic smell she'd caught between modules 4 and 5 — vegetal, living, out of place in this baked and arid terrain.

She thought about Maja sitting by the dry well because the ground was cooler. A woman who checked everything twice, three times. A woman who did research-level diagnostics on modules she was only meant to maintain. A woman whose death preceded an anomaly rather than caused one.

The numbers on the page were neat and small and meant something Amma did not yet understand.

She closed the notebook. Folded the separate sheet and slid it into the inner pocket of her bag, beneath the false bottom where her real credentials lived.

She lay down. The mattress dipped where Maja's body had worn it. Amma settled into the impression — hip, shoulder, the curve of the spine — and thought, not for the first time, that she was lying in a dead woman's bed following a dead woman's footsteps through a dead woman's work.

She counted heartbeats until the numbers lost their edges and sleep took her, and if she dreamed, she did not remember.
