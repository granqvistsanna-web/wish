# Chapter 3 — Module 7-12

The sand was cold through her boot soles.

Amma walked the path between the station buildings and the module field, counting steps the way she always did — a rhythm to hold the morning in place. Forty-seven from her door to the edge of the gravel. Sixty-three more to where the gravel gave way to packed sand. The air smelled of nothing yet. In an hour it would smell of heated metal and dust, but now it was just cold and clear, and her breath made faint shapes that disappeared before they fully formed.

The modules stretched south in long rows, squat rectangles angled toward a sun that hadn't cleared the horizon. From a distance they looked like stones placed by a methodical hand. Up close they were rougher — bolted panels, weather-stripped seams, sand drifted into corners. Each one hummed at a frequency she could feel in her chest before she could hear it.

Her shoulder ached. It always ached in the morning, the cold stiffening the scar tissue where the bone had healed wrong. She rolled it, felt the grind, stopped. Pain was information. You acknowledged it and moved on.

Module 7-12 was the third from the end of the seventh row. She'd memorized the grid from the station blueprints on the transport, back when this was still a production anomaly and a three-week assignment. The kind of job you filed a report on and forgot. That had been four days ago. She hadn't filed anything yet.

The module looked like every other one. Squat, rectangular, its solar array angled thirty-two degrees south-southwest. The identification plate bolted to the access panel read 7-12 in stamped characters, the paint faded but legible. Standard.

She crouched beside the access panel and ran her fingers along the seal.

Not standard.

The sealant was smooth — too smooth. Station-issue compound was rough, gritty, mixed with silica to withstand thermal expansion. This was commercial-grade. The kind you ordered from a supply catalog, not the kind you scraped out of a bucket in a maintenance shed. Someone had opened this panel and resealed it, and they'd used the wrong material to do it.

Amma photographed the seal with her datapad. Then she pulled her toolkit from her belt, selected the flat-edge driver, and cracked the panel open.

---

The service corridor inside Module 7-12 was barely wide enough for her shoulders. She turned sideways to move through the first section, one hand on the wall for balance. The hum was louder here, traveling through the metal and into her palm, her wrist, the bones of her forearm. The air was warm and close and smelled of heated circuits and old dust — the particular smell of machinery that had been running for years without a full shutdown.

She switched on her work light. The beam caught dust motes suspended in the still air.

Standard configuration for the first junction. Power conduits running left, data lines running right, everything color-coded and labeled in faded print. She checked the connection points, ran her scanner over the circuit boards. Readings normal. She moved deeper.

The second junction was where the official report placed the malfunction. Electrical fault in the control panel, causing the production drop. Simple. Fixable. Exactly the kind of thing that killed people when they got careless.

Amma wasn't careless.

She ran the scan three times. The readings didn't show a fault. They showed something else.

The output from Module 7-12 hadn't dropped. It had been redirected. A bypass had been installed — a clean, competent piece of work that rerouted energy from the main grid into a secondary circuit. The secondary circuit wasn't on any blueprint she'd been given. It fed into a conduit that went down, through the module's base, and into the ground.

She stared at her scanner readout for a long time.

This wasn't a malfunction. This was intentional. And whoever had done it knew exactly what they were doing.

Maja. It had to be Maja.

The woman who controlled everything twice, three times. The woman who wanted to fix it herself. Amma crouched in the narrow corridor and thought about a dead woman she'd never met, working here alone in this humming dark, hour after hour, building something that wasn't supposed to exist.

"Where does this go," Amma said. Her voice sounded flat in the enclosed space.

The conduit didn't answer.

---

She found the mark twenty minutes later.

She was checking the junction box behind the secondary circuit, tracing the conduit's path toward the module's base, when her light caught something on the inside of the rear panel. A scratch. Not damage from heat or friction — too regular. She leaned in.

A number, scratched into the bare metal with a sharp tool. The coating had been scraped away, leaving a raw, bright line against the dull surface.

13.

Below it, smaller, a series of characters. Letters. She couldn't read them clearly — the angle was wrong and the light insufficient. She pressed herself into the corner, turned the work light, and photographed it from three angles. The heat in the corridor was unbearable now, the sun having reached the module's exterior. Sweat ran from her hairline down the side of her face and dripped onto the panel.

She touched the scratch with her fingertip. The raw edge of the metal. Sharp enough to cut.

Maja had left this. Not in a log file or a report or a message — scratched into the inside of a machine, where only someone who came looking would find it. A woman who controlled everything twice, three times, had scratched a number into a panel in the dark and then gone out and died.

Amma's hands were not steady as she packed her tools.

---

The sun hit her like a wall when she climbed out of the module. She stood for a moment, blind, breathing the hot air that tasted of dust and ozone. The landscape swam — sand and modules and the low buildings of the station wavering in the heat like something not yet decided.

She walked back. The sun pressed on the back of her neck like a hand, steady and indifferent. Sweat cooled on her back and then heated again. She didn't count her steps this time.

She needed the communications system logs. The bypass installation had to have a timestamp — power fluctuations during the rerouting, access records, something. She needed to know when Maja had made those modifications. She needed to know if anyone else had accessed Module 7-12 after Maja's death and before Amma's arrival.

The station was quiet when she reached it. Early enough that most of the crew was still at breakfast or starting their morning routines. She could hear Viktor's voice from somewhere near the greenhouse, telling someone a story. The smell of coffee drifted from the kitchen.

She went past the kitchen to the communications room.

---

Samuel's space looked like Samuel. A shirt hung on a hook by the door. Two coffee cups on the console — one half-full, one empty, both with dark rings at the bottom. Loose papers with handwritten notes in a cramped, slanted script. The screens glowed blue in the dim room, cooler than the air outside by ten degrees at least. It smelled of coffee and unwashed cotton and something faintly chemical — the cleaner he used on the equipment, maybe.

Amma sat down at the secondary terminal and pulled up the maintenance logs for Module 7-12.

The records were there. Maja's access entries, beginning five months before her death. At first, once a week — routine checks consistent with her maintenance schedule. Then more frequently. Twice a week. Three times. In the final month, nearly every day. The entries were brief — timestamps and duration codes, no notes. Each one longer than the last. The final entry was the morning of the day she died. Three hours and forty-seven minutes inside the module.

Then nothing.

Amma scrolled through the subsequent entries. After Maja's death: nothing. No maintenance access to Module 7-12 for two weeks. Then a single entry from someone with an access code Amma didn't recognize — thirty minutes, no notes. Then nothing again until Amma's own access that morning.

She was reaching for the power fluctuation logs when she heard him.

Not footsteps. Not a sound, exactly. A shift in the air. The sense of the doorway being occupied.

"You're up early."

Samuel leaned against the doorframe. His hair was damp — he'd washed it, or tried to. The circles under his eyes were the same. He held a coffee cup in one hand, and the other hand was in his pocket. Relaxed. The crooked smile that didn't reach his eyes.

"Couldn't sleep," Amma said.

"The heat." He took a sip of coffee. "Or the footsteps. Did you hear them? Someone walks the perimeter every night. Karin, probably. She doesn't sleep either."

Amma said nothing. She had minimized the log files when she heard him, an instinct faster than thought. The screen now showed a standard maintenance dashboard. Nothing incriminating. Nothing interesting.

Samuel looked at the screen. Then at her. The smile shifted — narrowing, the chatter falling away like something shed.

"What are you looking for?"

"Production data. For the anomaly report."

"At six in the morning. In my chair."

"Your chair?"

"The one you're sitting in. It's my chair." He took another sip. "I don't mind. But I know what's on that dashboard and what isn't. And production data isn't something you'd get up before dawn to read."

Amma met his gaze. She could feel the distance between them — five steps, maybe six. The door open behind him. She could leave. She could deflect, make a joke, offer one of her safe truths. She could say she liked the smell of coffee in the morning.

"I'm looking at maintenance logs," she said.

"Maja's."

It wasn't a question.

"Yes."

Samuel was quiet for a moment. He took his hand out of his pocket and wrapped both hands around his coffee cup. The crooked smile was gone. What was underneath it was older, tireder, and much sharper.

"You're not a technician," he said. "I mean — you are. You know the work. Your hands are right for it. But you're not here to fix a production drop."

"I am here to fix a production drop."

"You're here because Maja died." He said it simply, the way you'd state the weather. "The Energy Department doesn't send technicians with your clearance level for a production anomaly at a station this small. I checked your access codes the night you arrived. Whatever grade you are, it's above maintenance."

The room was very quiet. The screens hummed. Outside, Viktor's voice had stopped.

"You've lied so long you've forgotten what's true," Samuel said. Not an accusation. The way he said it — flat, quiet, almost sad — made it worse. Like he was observing something familiar. Something he recognized.

Amma sat in his chair and looked at him and felt the careful architecture of Amma Wost — the cover, the performance, the counted steps and measured words — shift under her like sand.

She didn't say anything for a long time. Then: "What do you want?"

"I want to know what happened to Maja." His voice was steady. His hands around the cup were not. "She was my friend. Or she was something. We were both new here, the two of us, six years ago. And then she stopped talking to me and I never found out why, and then she died in a module she'd been working in alone for weeks, and the official report says electrical fault, and I know — I know — that's not what happened."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you're the first person who's come here who might actually look."

---

The truce was not spoken. It took shape in the silence after Samuel sat down across from her — not in his chair, which she still occupied, but in the spare chair by the wall. He put his coffee on the console between them. She noticed his scar: the pale line from his neck down over his shoulder, visible where his collar shifted. Old. Healed. The kind of mark that came from something you survived but didn't talk about.

"Maja used this system constantly in the last weeks," Samuel said. "Not just the maintenance logs. She was accessing files I didn't recognize. Technical specifications, schematics, something from the original construction archive. I asked her about it once. She gave me that look — the one that says the question is too stupid to answer."

"Did you report it?"

"She asked me not to." He turned the coffee cup in his hands. "And I didn't because — I don't know. Because she was Maja. Because she was the only person here who'd been here as long as I had. Because I thought she knew what she was doing."

Amma weighed her options. The bypass in Module 7-12. The scratch — the number thirteen, the letters below it. She could give him all of it. She could give him nothing.

She gave him one piece.

"Someone installed a bypass in Module 7-12," she said. "Energy from the main grid has been rerouted into a secondary circuit that doesn't appear on any blueprint. The production anomaly isn't a drop — it's a redirect."

Samuel's expression didn't change. But his hands went still around the cup.

"Into the ground?" he said.

She stared at him. "How did you know that?"

"Maja said something once. Near the end, when she was still talking to me. She said the station was built on top of something. I thought she was being dramatic."

The room was quiet again. The screens hummed their steady blue.

"The night before she died," Samuel said. His voice had changed — flatter, slower. The chatter was gone. His hands stayed still. "She came here. Late. Everyone else was asleep. She asked me to send a message. To someone outside — not through official channels. An encrypted burst to a relay point I'd never heard of."

"What was the message?"

"I don't know. I refused." He looked at his hands. "I said it was too risky. That the network was monitored. That if anyone at Capitol flagged it, we'd both be in the disciplinary queue." He paused. "She didn't argue. She just left. I thought she'd ask again the next day." Another pause, longer. "She was dead by afternoon."

Amma watched him. The stillness in his body was wrong — a man who couldn't sit still, sitting perfectly still. The grief was old enough to have calcified into something he wore, like the scar.

"You couldn't have known," she said. It was the obvious thing to say. She said it anyway.

"No." He picked up his coffee. "But I could have sent the message."

---

Amma left the communications room and walked to the kitchen.

The corridor between the two rooms was ten steps. She counted them. The habit was back, the counting — the scaffolding she built around herself to keep the shape of things manageable. Ten steps. A doorway. The kitchen.

Karin was there. Arms crossed — not defensively, just the way she stood, like a default setting. She was doing something at the counter. Slicing bread, maybe. She didn't turn when Amma came in.

"Eat," Karin said.

Amma sat at the table. Karin put bread in front of her, and a jar of preserved fruit — dark, sweet, something she didn't recognize — and a glass of water that tasted of mineral and cold stone.

She ate. The bread was dense and slightly sour. The fruit was sharp on her tongue. She was hungrier than she'd realized. The morning — the module, the bypass, the scratch, Samuel — had burned through whatever reserves the thin station breakfast had given her, and her body demanded replenishment with a force that overrode everything else.

Karin sat across from her. She didn't eat. She watched Amma eat, which should have been uncomfortable but wasn't. There was nothing predatory in it. Karin watched the way you watched bread rise — making sure the process was happening.

"The module," Karin said.

Amma looked up.

"You went out early." It wasn't a question either. People here didn't ask questions, she was learning. They made statements and let you decide what to do with them.

"I started the inspection."

"And?"

Amma chewed. Swallowed. "It's going to take more than three weeks."

Something shifted in Karin's face. Not a smile. A softening of something that was usually hard. "Good," she said. "Then you'll be here for a while."

She got up and went back to the counter. The conversation was over. It had lasted four sentences, and it was the most Karin had said to her at once.

---

Evening.

Amma sat on the edge of her bed with the datapad balanced on her knees. The room was dark except for the screen's blue glow. Outside, the station had settled into its nighttime sounds — someone in the kitchen, the distant clatter of dishes, Viktor coughing once, twice, then silence.

She pulled up the photographs from Module 7-12. The sealant. The bypass conduit. The junction box. She swiped through them methodically, making notes in a file she kept encrypted and off the station's network.

Then the scratch.

She zoomed in. The number was clear, even in the photograph. Thirteen. Cut deep enough to last, shallow enough not to damage the panel's function. Careful. Controlled twice, three times.

Below it, the letters. She increased the resolution, adjusted the contrast. In the enhanced image, the characters resolved.

Not coordinates. Not a code.

A name.

She read it twice and sat back against the wall. Her shoulder pressed into the concrete and she let it, let the ache ground her.

It was a name she knew. Not from the station, not from the crew files, not from the Energy Department briefing. From before. From the other side. A name that shouldn't be scratched inside a solar module on a farm at the edge of the desert.

Outside, the footsteps started. The same time as the night before. Steady, measured, circling the perimeter. They paused outside her window. She heard the sand compress under someone's weight, the small adjustment of a body standing still.

She didn't go to the window.

She turned off the datapad and lay in the dark and listened to whoever it was standing outside, and she counted their breaths by the silence between each soft compression of sand until they moved on, and then she lay there for a long time after, counting nothing, and the dark was very large and very close and she did not sleep.
