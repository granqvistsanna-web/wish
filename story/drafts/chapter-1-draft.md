# Chapter 1 — Arrival

The road was the color of bone.

Amma counted the kilometers on the dashboard display. Forty-three from the transfer depot, forty-three kilometers of nothing but flat scorched earth and the haze of heat above it. She counted because counting was what she did when the silence got too wide. Breaths. Steps. Seconds between one thought and the next.

The vehicle shuddered over a rut. Her shoulder hit the door frame and the pain was immediate, bright, familiar. She pressed her palm against the joint and held it there. Sektor 14. Three years gone and her body still carried it like a debt.

She let the pain pass. She'd learned to do that.

Station 7-Syd appeared first as a smudge. Then a collection of low buildings, the glint of greenhouses, and beyond them the module towers rising from the flats like teeth. Thirteen of them, spaced in a long row. Smaller than she'd expected. Everything out here was smaller than Capitol made it sound.

The vehicle stopped in a patch of shade that barely qualified. Amma took her bag and stepped out into the heat. It pressed against her face, her neck, the insides of her wrists. Forty-one degrees, maybe forty-two. The air tasted like dust and warm metal.

No one was waiting except a woman leaning against the nearest doorframe with her arms crossed. Not defensively. Like she'd been standing that way for years and saw no reason to change.

"Come in out of the sun," the woman said. Her voice was short and rough, as if she'd just woken up or smoked too many cigarettes. She didn't offer her hand. She turned and walked inside.

Amma followed.

---

The woman was Karin. She didn't give a title. "I manage the place," she said. "Like a caretaker, roughly."

She showed Amma the room first. Small, clean, a single bed pushed against the wall. A window that faced the module field. A shelf with three hooks below it. The air inside was cooler but still heavy.

"This was Maja's," Karin said. It wasn't a question and it wasn't an apology. Just information.

The mattress held the faint impression of another body. Amma set her bag on the floor.

Karin stood in the doorway and looked at her. It was a specific kind of looking — not hostile, not curious. Assessing. Like she was checking a gauge.

"You're from the other side," Karin said.

Amma's hands went still. She'd been reaching for the window latch. She stopped.

"Your skin," Karin said. "The way you stand. Capitol people don't hold their weight like that."

Amma said nothing. Her pulse counted for her: one, two, three, four.

"I wouldn't mention it to the others," Karin said. She pushed off the doorframe. "Kitchen's at the end of the hall. Dinner at six. Eat."

She was gone before Amma could decide what to say.

---

Viktor found her before she found him. He appeared at the edge of the greenhouse path, white mustache, wide stride, hands that trembled slightly at his sides. He walked like a man who had once been large and was settling into the memory of it.

"The new technician," he said. Not a question either. People here didn't seem to ask much. "Come. I'll show you everything."

Everything meant the station according to Viktor. The water reclamation system he'd helped install. The greenhouse he'd built from salvaged panels. The generator shed where he kept his tools in the same order as twenty years ago. He talked with the precision of a man narrating his own monument.

"I built half of this with these hands," he said, holding them up. They shook. He didn't seem to notice. "When there was nothing here but sand and wind and goats."

"How long?" Amma asked.

"Long enough." He pointed toward the module towers. Thirteen columns of dull metal against the white sky. "We produce thirteen percent of the front's energy. From this." He swept his hand across the view. "From nothing."

She doubted the number. She said nothing.

"How many years of experience do you have?" Viktor asked.

"Eight," Amma said.

"Two?"

"Eight."

"Seven?"

"No. Eight."

Viktor squinted at her. "You seem young for that."

She was. That was one of the things she lied about.

They came around the corner of the machine shed and nearly walked into a man sitting on an overturned crate, cleaning a circuit relay with a rag. He looked up. Blond hair too long and in need of washing. Dark circles under his eyes. A crooked smile that surfaced immediately, reflexively, like a defense mechanism.

"You must be the replacement," he said.

"Amma," she said. She didn't offer her hand either. She was learning.

"Samuel. I run the comms." He stood, tucking the rag into his pocket. His shirt shifted and Amma saw the scar — a pale line running from his neck down over his shoulder, old and healed but unmistakable. She looked away before he caught her looking.

"Samuel talks too much," Viktor said. "Everyone agrees."

"Everyone agrees because Cassia said it once and Cassia is always right," Samuel said. The smile again. "You'll like it here. Everyone does, eventually." A pause. "Except the ones who don't."

His eyes stayed on her face a beat too long. Not unfriendly. Searching.

Amma excused herself to unpack. She had nothing to unpack. She sat on Maja's mattress and counted the tiles on the ceiling. Twenty-seven. She counted them again.

---

Dinner was at a long table in the kitchen. Plain food: bread, soup thick with root vegetables, a slab of something fermented that Amma didn't ask about. The room was warm and low-ceilinged, with a row of windows that let in the last of the daylight.

She took a seat near the end and watched.

Viktor claimed the head of the table by habit and volume. He was telling Samuel about a repair he'd done in the early days, back when the modules ran on a different system. Samuel listened with the appearance of interest and the reality of having heard it before.

Karin sat across from Amma. She ate the way she talked — efficiently, without waste. She did not look at Amma again.

Two people Amma hadn't met sat together on the far side. So alike it took her a moment to find the differences. The man had a slightly narrower face. The woman had darker hair. They ate in synchronized rhythm, shoulders almost touching.

"The twins," Samuel said, catching her glance. "Elias and Nora. Maintenance."

Elias looked up. Nora didn't. Their curiosity was quiet and methodical, like scientists observing a specimen. If they hoped to learn something from her, they could keep hoping.

There was another person. A woman, dark features, her black hair pulled into a tight knot that caught the light. She sat at the far end of the table and ate slowly, methodically, one small bite at a time. She did not look up from her plate. She did not speak. Not once during the entire meal.

"Li," Samuel said. He offered nothing else. For once, he didn't fill the silence.

Li's quietness wasn't shy. It wasn't hostile. It was absolute, like a shape cut from the center of the conversation. Everyone talked around it. No one tried to include her. Amma noted this the way she noted everything — as data, as a map of what was permitted here.

There was an empty chair. Between Karin and the wall, the wood a shade lighter than the others, as though someone had recently scrubbed it. No one sat in it. No one mentioned it.

Amma looked at the chair and then she didn't.

---

The room changed when Cassia walked in.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. The way air pressure shifts before rain. Viktor straightened in his seat. Karin looked up from her plate. Li's gaze lifted for one second, then dropped back.

Cassia was in her fifties, or near it. Her hair was long, curling, gray at the temples and dark everywhere else, pulled up in a loose knot that was coming undone. Her face was the kind you couldn't stop looking at — not because it was beautiful by any conventional standard, but because it was entirely present. Everything she looked at, she looked at completely. When those light eyes landed on Amma, Amma felt the full weight of someone's attention for the first time in years.

"I'm sorry," Cassia said, sitting down. "The south modules needed recalibrating." She served herself soup without ceremony. She wore the same work clothes as everyone else. On her they looked deliberate.

"You're the technician from the department," she said to Amma. It was warm. Direct. She said it the way you'd greet someone you'd been waiting for.

"Amma Wost."

"Cassia." Just the first name. No title. "Maja's replacement. Welcome." She tore a piece of bread. Chewed. Swallowed. "She was good."

A small silence. Viktor filled it by passing the fermented slab to no one in particular. Samuel caught it.

Cassia asked about the journey. About the department. About Amma's training. Standard questions delivered with genuine interest. Amma gave the answers she'd practiced on the road. Clean, consistent, boring. Cassia received each one without challenge. Her eyes said she was filing every word.

After the meal, Samuel and Viktor fell into argument about the water pressure in the second greenhouse. The twins cleared their plates and vanished. Li stood, placed her chair against the wall with both hands, and left without a word. Karin began washing dishes. The station's evening routine closed around the newcomer like water over a stone.

---

Cassia caught her in the hallway.

"Walk with me?"

The station at twilight. The heat was fading but the ground still radiated warmth through the soles of Amma's boots. The sky was enormous — wider than any sky in Capitol, layered in colors she didn't have names for. The modules stood black against the horizon.

Cassia talked about the farm. How it ran, what it needed. Thirteen modules, each requiring regular maintenance. Energy production was steady but aging. The infrastructure Viktor had built was remarkable but patched together, and Maja had been the one keeping it functional.

"The department sent their investigation report after she died," Cassia said. "Three pages. An electrical fault in the control panel at module 7-12. Case closed."

Amma walked beside her and said nothing.

"If you find anything unusual in the modules," Cassia said, "I'd like you to tell me first. Before you put it in any report."

There it was. Amma heard it the way she heard all wrong things — as a specific frequency, a note out of key. A station commander asking a subordinate to withhold information from the department. It was improper at best. At worst, it was obstruction.

"I know how that sounds," Cassia said. She stopped walking. They stood in the near-dark, the module towers behind them. "But Maja checked everything twice. Three times. I don't understand what happened to her. And the department's three pages didn't help me understand."

Amma looked at her. In the dying light, Cassia's face was half-visible, half-shadow. Her expression was open and steady and Amma could not tell if it was genuine.

"I'll keep you informed," Amma said. Careful. Noncommittal.

Cassia nodded. Her hand touched Amma's arm — brief, warm, just above the elbow. "Thank you. Sleep well."

She walked back toward the station. Amma stood in the dark and counted to sixty before she followed.

---

The room was Maja's. The bed was Maja's. The indentation in the mattress was the shape of a woman Amma had never met and would not meet. She lay on her back and the shoulder ground against the mattress and the pain was steady, patient, an old companion.

She counted ceiling tiles. Twenty-seven. She counted them again. Twenty-seven.

She thought about her mother. The Wall in the dark, rain hammering the earth to mud, and her mother's arms tight around something small and quiet. A baby who didn't cry. Amma had been told this story by the woman who raised her in Capitol, who'd found the baby at a relief shelter and taken it as her own. Her mother had not made it through. The official record said nothing about that night because officially it never happened.

She thought about Karin's flat assessment. *You're from the other side.* And then the warning. *I wouldn't mention it to the others.* Which meant Karin saw what others might see. Which meant the cover was already thinner than she needed it to be.

She thought about Cassia's hand on her arm.

She pushed that thought aside and replaced it with the mission. Module 7-12. The production anomaly. The three-page investigation. That was why she was here. She would do the job, file the report, and leave. She would not be drawn into whatever this place was. She had done this before.

The shoulder pulsed. She counted the pulses. She stopped counting.

She got up. Her feet were bare on the cool floor. She walked to the kitchen for water.

On the shelf beside the stove, between a tin of dried mint and a cracked clay pot, sat a coffee cup. It was unremarkable — thick ceramic, a chip on the rim, a ring of dried brown inside. It sat where someone had placed it and no one had moved it. The dust on its surface was even, undisturbed.

Maja's cup. It had to be. The one nobody used and nobody put away. It sat on the shelf like a held breath.

Amma drank her water. She looked at the cup. She went back to bed.

She didn't sleep for a long time.
