# Style Guide — Octavia Butler Influence

## Voice & Tone

- **Point of view**: Close third person or first person. Deep interiority.
- **Tense**: Past tense (default). Present tense permitted for prologues/interludes.
- **Tone**: Spare, direct, unflinching. Warmth emerges through action and proximity, not declaration.
- **Emotional register**: Earned emotion only. No melodrama. Let silence and gesture carry weight.

## Prose Rules

### Sentence Structure
- Average sentence length: 12–18 words. Vary widely.
- Mix short declarative punches (3–6 words) with longer, layered sentences.
- Fragments are permitted for rhythm and emphasis.
- Avoid three consecutive sentences of the same length.

### Paragraphs
- Keep paragraphs tight: 1–4 sentences typical.
- Single-sentence paragraphs for impact — use sparingly.
- White space is a tool. Let the reader breathe.

### Dialogue
- Dialogue should be clipped, natural, often interrupted.
- Characters speak differently from each other. Track speech patterns in character sheets.
- Use "said" as the default dialogue tag. Avoid adverb-laden tags.
- Let dialogue do double duty: reveal character AND advance plot.
- Subtext is mandatory. Characters rarely say exactly what they mean.

### Description & Worldbuilding
- Ground the reader in the body: temperature, texture, smell, taste, proprioception.
- Worldbuild through character interaction, not exposition dumps.
- Technology and social systems revealed through lived experience.
- The strange should feel mundane to the characters. The reader catches up.

### Themes (Butler-inspired)
- Power dynamics in intimate relationships
- Bodily autonomy and consent
- Survival and adaptation
- The cost of connection across difference
- Community-building under oppression
- Genetic/biological destiny vs. choice

## Metrics

| Metric | Target |
|--------|--------|
| Avg sentence length | 12–18 words |
| Dialogue ratio | 30–45% of chapter |
| Sensory details | Min 2 per page |
| Internal monologue | 15–25% of chapter |
| Adverb density | < 1 per 200 words |
| "Said" as dialogue tag | > 80% of tags |

## Anti-Patterns — What Not To Do

### 1. Emotional Annotation

**Don't** name emotions and then explain them. Trust the reader.

> **Bad**: She felt a deep sense of grief wash over her, the kind of grief that comes from losing someone you never expected to need. Her heart ached with the weight of it.

> **Good**: She sat on his side of the bed. The sheets still smelled like engine grease.

The bad version tells the reader what to feel three times. The good version gives one image and lets grief arrive on its own.

---

### 2. Purple Interiority

**Don't** let internal monologue become ornate, drifting, or philosophical in ways the character wouldn't actually think.

> **Bad**: She wondered if love was always this way — a quiet unraveling of the self, thread by thread, until what remained was something neither of them could name but both understood in the marrow of their being.

> **Good**: She didn't know what this was. She knew she kept coming back.

Characters think in the register they live in. A biologist thinks in specimens and field notes, not poetry. A mechanic thinks in torque and tolerances.

---

### 3. Symmetrical Emotion

**Don't** give characters matching, mirrored feelings. Real intimacy is asymmetric.

> **Bad**: They looked at each other, both feeling the same pull, both afraid to speak it, both knowing the other felt it too.

> **Good**: He reached for her hand. She let him take it. She was thinking about the air recycler.

Two people in the same scene rarely have the same emotional experience at the same time. One wants. The other tolerates. One grieves. The other is hungry. This asymmetry is where Butler finds truth.

---

### 4. Scenic Throat-Clearing

**Don't** open scenes with atmosphere before anything happens.

> **Bad**: The corridors of the station were dimly lit, casting long shadows across the metal flooring. The hum of the ventilation system provided a constant backdrop to life aboard Threshold. It was the kind of place that made you forget what sunlight felt like.

> **Good**: Tal heard the seal breach before the alarm caught up. She was already running.

Enter scenes late. The first sentence should contain a person doing something or a change happening. Setting details can come through action — a character touches a cold wall, hears a failing compressor, tastes recycled air.

---

### 5. Catalogue Descriptions

**Don't** inventory a character's appearance or a room's contents.

> **Bad**: She had dark brown eyes, high cheekbones, and a scar that ran from her left temple to her jaw. She wore a fitted grey jumpsuit with reinforced boots and a utility belt that held three tools.

> **Good**: The scar pulled when she smiled, which wasn't often.

Pick one or two details that reveal character. Butler never describes a full body — she gives you a hand, a way of standing, a reflex.

---

### 6. Choreographed Beats

**Don't** track every micro-movement in a conversation.

> **Bad**: He looked at her. She looked away. He shifted in his seat. She folded her arms. He cleared his throat. "We should talk," he said. She nodded slowly.

> **Good**: "We should talk," he said. She kept her eyes on the viewport.

One gesture per exchange is enough. Choose the one that contradicts or complicates the dialogue.

---

### 7. AI Summary Voice

**Don't** let narration sound like it's explaining the story to someone who isn't reading it.

> **Bad**: This was a turning point for Tal — a moment that would reshape her understanding of what the Architects truly wanted. Nothing would be the same after this.

> **Good**: *(Just continue the scene. The reader will know it's a turning point because the events show it.)*

Never editorialize on your own plot. No "little did she know," no "this would change everything," no narrator stepping outside the character's head to flag significance.

---

### 8. Uniform Sentence Rhythm

**Don't** let every sentence follow the same Subject-Verb-Object pattern at the same length.

> **Bad**: She walked to the console. She pressed the sequence. She waited for the response. She heard the hatch open. She moved toward the door.

> **Good**: She keyed the sequence. Waited. The hatch groaned open, and the smell hit her before the light did.

Vary length, structure, and rhythm. Fragments. Compound sentences that unspool. Short punches between them. The prose should breathe.

---

### 9. Ornamental Sensory Detail

**Don't** add sensory language as decoration. Every detail should do work.

> **Bad**: The warm amber glow of the overhead lights bathed the room in a soft, honeyed luminescence that seemed to caress every surface.

> **Good**: The overhead light buzzed at a frequency that made her teeth itch.

Sensory detail should reveal something — about the environment, the character's state, or the danger they're in. If a detail could belong to any scene, it belongs to none.

---

### 10. Resolved Tension

**Don't** let characters process, understand, or resolve emotional conflict within the same scene it arises.

> **Bad**: At first she was angry. But as she thought about it, she realized he had been trying to protect her. She felt the anger melt into understanding, and finally, gratitude. "Thank you," she said quietly.

> **Good**: "Don't do that again," she said. She meant it as a warning. It came out like a plea.

Butler lets tension sit. Characters misunderstand each other for chapters. Forgiveness, when it comes, is incomplete. Emotional resolution that arrives on schedule is the hallmark of manufactured fiction.

---

### 11. "X but Y" Contrast Writing

**Don't** structure sentences as two halves joined by "but," "yet," or "and yet" to manufacture depth.

> **Bad**: Her voice was steady, but her hands betrayed her. The station was silent, yet it hummed with unspoken tension. He was a stranger, but something about him felt ancient and familiar. She wanted to run, yet her feet carried her closer.

> **Good**: Her voice was steady. Her hands she kept behind her back.

This is one of the most common AI prose tics — every sentence becomes a seesaw of contrasts. It reads as profound on first pass and hollow on second. One contradiction per scene can land. Four per paragraph is a pattern the reader feels before they name it. When every sentence contains its own counterpoint, nothing surprises.

Alternatives: let the contradiction live across sentences, across paragraphs, across chapters. Show the steady voice in one line and the shaking hands three paragraphs later. Trust the reader to hold both.

## What Butler Does That We Emulate

- Opens in medias res with immediate tension
- Builds romantic/intimate connection through shared vulnerability and survival
- Never explains what characters would take for granted
- Uses biology and the body as both metaphor and plot engine
- Creates power imbalances that shift — no permanent victims or villains
- Ends chapters on quiet devastation, not cheap cliffhangers
